# Bus connection policy

What `src/core/bus.py` hands every caller a client with, and why each value is what it is (#287, closing CannObserv/broker#1 R7). Split out of [ARCHITECTURE.md](ARCHITECTURE.md) — the bus *topology* stays there; this is the transport underneath it.

Three of the values rest on measurements that contradicted the intuition going in, so each is quoted where it applies rather than summarised here. Archiver's half of R7 landed in CannObserv/archiver#193 Phase 1 (`src/core/changes/bus_client.py`), where all three were taken.

## What the policy replaced

`bus_client_from_env()` was a bare `Redis.from_url` with library defaults, which loopback made harmless: a local broker either answers in microseconds or refuses immediately. Neither holds once the broker moves to its own node — the measured path from this VM to `co-broker` is **~40 ms, DERP-relayed, and it never establishes a direct connection** (24 pings across two runs, all `direct connection not established`, despite `UDP: true` and `MappingVariesByDestIP: false` here; `tailscale netcheck` puts watcher's nearest DERP at `lax` while the broker is in `pdx`, so the number is cross-region and re-measurable after this VM's own move). A *stalled* broker — as distinct from a refusing one — would hold a read open with no bound, and `get_shared_bus_client` pins one client for the process, so the wedge sits inside a service whose `/health` is unauthenticated and DB-free by contract and knows nothing about the bus. The policy lives on the **single construction funnel** (`src/core/bus.py`), so shared, injected and script-owned clients all carry it; the #262 gate above is unchanged and still decides *whether* a client is built at all.

**`socket_timeout` has a floor, not a ceiling — this is the counter-intuitive one.** redis-py does **not** extend it for a blocking command: measured on redis-py 7.4.1, a client at `socket_timeout=1` issuing `XREAD … BLOCK 3000` raised `TimeoutError` after 1.01 s, while the same call at `socket_timeout=10` returned normally after 3.08 s. Both Watcher loops block for 5 s, so any value at or below that does not bound a stall — it *manufactures* one on every idle read against a perfectly healthy broker, leaving two consumers spinning on spurious timeouts. So the floor is the longest `BLOCK_MS` of any loop on the client, and the value is **derived** from it rather than transcribed beside a comment naming it. The windows live in a leaf module, `src/core/read_windows.py`, which the workers re-export as their own `BLOCK_MS`: `src/core/bus.py` must read them, and importing `src/workers/` to do so would be a genuine cycle (`registry_reconcile` → `watch_status` → `core.bus`), not a style preference. `tests/core/test_bus_client_policy.py` **discovers** every `BLOCK_MS` under `src/` rather than listing the two known today — Watcher's two windows are in different modules and can drift apart — and audits the leaf's claim to hold the longest against that scan. The scan is a recursive *text* pass over `src/**/*.py` that then imports only the modules mentioning the name: `pkgutil.iter_modules` is non-recursive and was scoped to one package, so a consumer added under a subpackage (or anywhere in `src/core/`, which has five) defined a window the guard could not see while still reading as complete — and a regex precise enough to skip comments would have missed a `from … import X as BLOCK_MS` alias. Over-matching costs one wasted import; under-matching costs the whole invariant.

**`socket_connect_timeout` bounds one attempt, not the call.** The budget an operator actually waits is `(retries + 1) × socket_connect_timeout`. Measured against a black-holed address on redis-py 7.4.1: `connect=5/retries=0` raised at 5.01 s, `connect=5/retries=1` at 10.03 s, `connect=2/retries=1` at 4.02 s. `WORST_CASE_CONNECT_SECONDS` keeps that expressed as the product even though the factor is 1 today, because it is what makes a retry added later cost twice what its own diff appears to say.

**Zero retries, stated rather than inherited.** A redis-py retry **re-sends the command**; it does not resume the response — `Redis.execute_command` wraps `_send_command_parse_response` in `Retry.call_with_retry`, so a `TimeoutError` raised *after* the broker already applied an `XADD` publishes the entry a second time. Watcher's exposure is on the producer side. Duplicates on the two LWW config/state streams (`content.fetch-policy`, `info.watch-status`) are absorbed by construction, but **`content.fetch` is a command stream with a consumer group, and a duplicated command is a duplicated fetch** — a second real origin request against a government portal under Watcher's pinned User-Agent. Nothing is given up by declining it: both consumer loops back off and retry on any error, and the fetch-command outbox is the durable buffer behind the publisher.

**What `health_check_interval` actually buys, stated precisely.** It does *not* heal a dead session invisibly — `check_health` runs its PING through the **connection's** retry object, which is this one, so at zero retries a silently dropped session reaches the caller as a `TimeoutError` either way. What moves is **when**. Measured through a proxy dropping only the replies — the relay-timeout shape, where the command lands and the answer never returns:

| | client sees | broker applied the `XADD` |
|---|---|---|
| `health_check_interval=30` | `TimeoutError` on the PING | **0** — never written |
| no health check | `TimeoutError` on the `XADD` | **1** — the ambiguous state |

So the two settings are complementary rather than alternatives: the interval shrinks the window in which an ambiguous outcome is possible, and the zero guarantees that whatever is left of that window never becomes a second `XADD`. A cleanly *closed* connection needs neither — redis-py notices the FIN and reconnects through it transparently at zero retries (measured with `CLIENT KILL`, interval on and off). Only the silent drop needs the PING. **The library's own default depends on which constructor is used, and only one of the two is zero.** On the `from_url` path this module takes, the connection-level default is `Retry(NoBackoff(), 0)` — an object that reads as a policy while behaving as none, and `retry_on_timeout=True` raises it to one while registering `TimeoutError` twice. But `Redis(host=…)` defaults to `Retry(ExponentialWithJitterBackoff(base=1, cap=10), retries=3)`, so a future switch to the constructor form would silently acquire three retries with jitter — exactly the hazard above. The zero is therefore passed explicitly, making it a decision that has to be argued with on either path, and a test pins the asymmetry so the warning cannot quietly become false. The `ExponentialBackoff` handed to it is never consulted at zero retries and is stated anyway: it decides the shape if `BUS_RETRIES` is ever raised, where a bare `NoBackoff()` would silently mean *re-send immediately*. `retry_on_error` is deliberately **not** passed: redis-py's `Retry` already carries exactly `(ConnectionError, TimeoutError)`, and repeating the pair would imply this widens something it does not.

**A startup PING, because `from_url` is lazy.** It returns against a broker with nothing listening and raises nothing, so no `try/except` around the lifespan's wiring can see an unreachable broker: the process starts, both consumers are scheduled, and the first failure lands inside a loop that correctly treats it as transient and backs off quietly. That is right for a partition and indistinguishable from an idle cluster for a misconfiguration — and Watcher makes the reading worse than Archiver's, since with no fact inbox nothing can complete and "quiet" is also what a healthy idle Watcher looks like. So the lifespan spawns one `probe_bus_reachable` that logs ERROR when the PING fails and INFO with the round trip when it succeeds. **A transport failure is retried for `BOOT_REACHABILITY_WINDOW_SECONDS` (30 s) before it is reported (#296)** — the window bounds when the last attempt starts, and that attempt runs to its own connect and read timeouts, so a black-holed broker reports up to one attempt later. On a dedicated VM a cold boot races tailnet DNS — replicator#88 found the tailnet address local while MagicDNS still answered `EAI_NODATA` for `broker` — and a single PING there would log "the bus is NOT idle, it is down" on every reboot. Only transport errors wait (`ConnectionError`, `TimeoutError`, which includes `BusyLoadingError`); `AuthenticationError`/`AuthorizationError` are excluded by name despite subclassing `ConnectionError`, since waiting cannot fix a credential. The consumers back off on their own meanwhile, so the window delays the diagnosis, never the recovery. The unit orders `After=tailscaled.service` for the half systemd can do — ordering only, never `Wants=`/`Requires=`. **Detached**, held on `app.state.bus_reachability_task` and cancelled at shutdown: even `WORST_CASE_CONNECT_SECONDS` of a blocked lifespan is time the dashboard does not serve, and the dashboard has no business being unavailable because the bus is. The URL is emitted with every credential it can carry redacted (`redact_url`, failing *closed* on a URL it cannot parse) — the broker gains a `requirepass` at CannObserv/broker#1 D3 and this line goes to journald.

**A redis URL carries a credential in two places, not one.** Besides the userinfo section, `from_url` parses query arguments straight into connection kwargs, so `redis://broker:6379/0?password=…` and `rediss://…?ssl_password=…` are legal forms — and redacting only userinfo let both through verbatim, an open failure in the one control whose whole job is to fail closed. Both are redacted, matched by *shape* (any parameter whose name contains `password`) rather than by a literal pair, so a future redis-py that adds another `*_password` is covered on arrival. A URL carrying neither is returned byte-identical, which is why the netloc is reused verbatim when only the query needed rewriting.

**The loops need no transient/poison classification of their own.** Both catch every non-`CancelledError` and back off — `tests/workers/test_bus_reconnect.py` pins that against `ConnectionError`, `TimeoutError`, the `NoPermissionError` that arrives once broker#1 D3 puts an ACL in front of the broker (a `ResponseError` subclass, not a connection error, so a loop classifying by type would be the one to get it wrong), and `OutOfMemoryError` (#288, same shape from a different cause). Every prior loop-failure test drives a *handler* failure raised inside `process_fact_message` / `reconcile_announcement`; a broker error is raised by `read` itself, one frame further out, and nothing exercised that path.

**"Catch everything" was one step short of true, and the step was structural (#289).** The blobs loop's undecodable-frame handler acks past the frame, and that ack ran in a *sibling* `except` clause — so the `except Exception` below it could not catch it, whatever it caught. An undecodable frame plus any broker failure on the `XACK` terminated the task, taking the fact inbox with it until a restart while `/health` stayed green. Both halves are individually ordinary, which is why nothing noticed: the branch exists *for* undecodable frames, and an ack failure is the exact class the backoff was written to absorb. It now has its own guard, and the frame simply comes back to be acked again. The registry loop's equivalent branch is fine as it stands — it advances a local cursor (`seek`) rather than issuing a command — so the asymmetry is deliberate and not a gap.

## Behaviour under `OOM command not allowed` (#288)

The broker runs `maxmemory-policy noeviction` under an explicit `maxmemory 512mb`, which converts an unbounded stream into a bounded, retryable error — but the blast radius is **instance-wide**: once the cap is reached, `XADD` is refused for every producer on the broker, not just the one that filled it. So the cap only buys what it claims if every client treats the refusal as transient. Watcher's behaviour was unverified from the epic's opening until #288; what follows was measured, not read, against a scratch `redis-server 7.0.15` started as `redis-server --port 6399 --maxmemory 1mb --maxmemory-policy noeviction --appendonly no` — never against `co-broker`, whose cap is instance-wide and carries production traffic for three services. Recorded for CannObserv/broker#6 (the OOM contract) and CannObserv/broker#2 (the ACL users). The regression guards are `tests/workers/test_broker_oom.py`, the `oom` parametrisations in `tests/workers/test_source_revisions_drain.py` and `tests/workers/test_bus_reconnect.py`, and the dead-letter rule in `tests/test_bus_stream_kinds.py`.

**The error is a `ResponseError`, not a connection error** — `redis.exceptions.OutOfMemoryError`, message `command not allowed when used memory > 'maxmemory'.`, MRO `OutOfMemoryError → ResponseError → RedisError`. That is the whole trap: a producer whose transient set is the intuitive `(ConnectionError, TimeoutError)` sorts a bounded, self-healing refusal into the permanent bucket and dead-letters valid events. `src/workers/source_revisions_drain.py` is the only producer here that classifies at all, and it names `OutOfMemoryError` explicitly in `_TRANSIENT_PUBLISH_ERRORS` (`NoPermissionError` joins it below); the other three do not classify, which is why they cannot get it wrong.

**Nothing is dropped and nothing is dead-lettered.** `content.fetch` — the one that matters most, because it is a command stream where a lost publish is a fetch that never happens and nothing downstream notices the absence — leaves the row `pending_publish` on all three of its publish paths (`_issue_fetch_command`, the every-minute sweep, the reaper's `_reissue`), each of which catches broadly and logs. There is **no attempt counter on that outbox and therefore no ceiling**, and the reaper scans `IN_FLIGHT` only, so an outage longer than `WATCHER_FETCH_COMMAND_TIMEOUT_SECONDS` does not quietly garbage-collect the backlog the sweep is still retrying; recovery is the same row under the same `command_id`, which Replicator dedupes. `content.revisions` retries on its 60 s → 1 h backoff, exempt from `MAX_PUBLISH_ATTEMPTS` because the ceiling applies to the non-transient branch only. `content.fetch-policy` and `info.watch-status` have no outbox by design: the publish raises, Procrastinate records the failed job, and the next tick republishes the whole set — the full-set republish *is* the recovery, so a batch that aborted partway is corrected within one period. Retried indefinitely, in every case.

**Only two of the commands Watcher issues are `denyoom`**, which is what keeps the consumers alive. Measured with the cap latched (fill, then halve `maxmemory` so no single command drops back under it — see below):

| Command | Watcher's use | Under the cap |
|---|---|---|
| `XADD <topic>` | all four producers | **refused** — `denyoom` |
| `XADD <topic>.dlq` | the dead-letter seam | **refused** — `denyoom` |
| `XGROUP CREATE` | `ensure_group` at consumer boot | **refused** — `denyoom`, even where the group already exists (the `BUSYGROUP` reply never happens; the OOM check runs first) |
| `XREADGROUP` / `XACK` / `XAUTOCLAIM` | the `content.blobs` loop | allowed |
| `XREAD` | the `info.registry` tail reader | allowed (`readonly`) |
| `XLEN` / `PING` | health, probe | allowed |

So the `content.blobs` consumer keeps draining its backlog throughout — it reads, processes and acks normally — and the groupless `info.registry` reader is untouched. **Neither loop wedges.** The one path that does fire is `ensure_group`, and only on a **boot** that lands inside the cap: it sits inside the backoff guard (#287 CR-12), so the loop logs, waits 5 s and retries until the cap clears rather than dying before its first read. The cost is that the fact inbox stalls for the duration of a restart-during-OOM — bounded, visible, and self-healing, but worth knowing before diagnosing a quiet Watcher as idle.

**The OOM state is a threshold, not a latch, and that matters for anyone reproducing this.** Refusal happens only while `used_memory > maxmemory` at the instant the command is processed, so at the boundary a single 4 KB entry flips the instance in and out of refusal — a naive fill-then-probe script observes `XADD` succeeding two commands after it was refused. Fill until the first refusal, then lower `maxmemory` below the current `used_memory`, and the state holds still long enough to probe.

**The dead-letter write, observed rather than inferred.** broker#2's ACL draft grants `~<topics>.dlq` on the reading that `co_core_aio.bus.dead_letter` issues `XADD <topic>.dlq`; neither `MONITOR` capture the draft was built from ever exercised it. Captured here against the scratch broker, the seam issues **two** commands, and the second lands on the *original* stream:

```
"XADD" "content.blobs.dlq" "*" <fields…>
"XACK" "content.blobs" "watcher.blobs" "<message-id>"
```

The full inventory the same capture produced, which is what an ACL user for Watcher has to cover: `XADD content.fetch`, `XADD content.revisions`, `XADD content.fetch-policy MAXLEN ~ <n>`, `XADD info.watch-status MAXLEN ~ <n>`, `XGROUP CREATE content.blobs watcher.blobs $ MKSTREAM`, `XREADGROUP GROUP watcher.blobs watcher-blobs-1 COUNT n BLOCK ms STREAMS content.blobs >`, `XAUTOCLAIM content.blobs watcher.blobs watcher-blobs-1 0 0-0 COUNT n`, `XACK content.blobs watcher.blobs <id>`, `XREAD [BLOCK ms] COUNT n STREAMS info.registry 0-0`, plus `PING`.

**Watcher writes no dead letter today, and the grant is for a path with no caller.** `content.blobs.dlq` is Watcher's under broker#1 Phase 5, but the loop's current poison-frame policy is to ack past an undecodable frame: on a fact stream read with our own group there is no correlation obligation to discharge and no DLQ of ours to route to (`src/workers/fetch_facts.py`). A rule in `tests/test_bus_stream_kinds.py` — beside the other source-scanning taxonomy guards, on the same AST scanner — fails the moment either spelling of a DLQ write appears under `src/` or `scripts/`: the co-core seam `consumer.dead_letter(…)`, or a hand-rolled `xadd(dlq_name(topic), …)` that bypasses it. So the sentence above cannot go stale silently. One asymmetry for whoever builds that policy: reading and acking survive the cap but quarantining does not, so the DLQ write is itself a retryable step, never one that always succeeds.

## Behaviour under `NOPERM` (#290)

broker#1 D3 puts per-service ACL users in front of the broker (broker#2), and a rule that is mistyped or too narrow denies `XADD` on a perfectly valid row. **`NoPermissionError` is a `ResponseError` subclass too** — disjoint from the connection errors, exactly the OOM trap from a different cause — and it is an operator-resolvable outage, not poison: the event publishes unchanged the moment the rule is widened. So `_TRANSIENT_PUBLISH_ERRORS` names it, on the same argument #288 accepted for `OutOfMemoryError`. Nothing here was measured; the ACL users are not deployed. Guards: `tests/workers/test_broker_acl.py` plus the `noperm` parametrisations in `tests/workers/test_source_revisions_drain.py` and `tests/workers/test_bus_reconnect.py`.

**The missing entry cost little, which is why #290 was filed low and not as a blocker on broker#2.** No data: `MAX_PUBLISH_ATTEMPTS = 100_000` against a 60 s → 1 h backoff is on the order of a decade, so the dead-letter branch was unreachable in practice — unlike CannObserv/replicator#82, where the same gap burns a real delivery ceiling. What it cost was the **log field**: `"transient": false` on the one an operator greps to tell a config problem from a poison row, at the cutover, when the errors are already unfamiliar. Plus the dead-letter branch itself, correct in principle and unreachable in fact.

**What the entry does *not* buy is a faster recovery, and #290's own text says otherwise — it is wrong.** `mark_failure` runs on **both** branches of the classifier (`src/workers/source_revisions_drain.py`), so the exponential backoff to the 3600 s cap is identical whether a refusal is filed transient or not. Classification decides the log field and the ceiling; it does not decide the schedule. An operator who widens the rule still waits out the row's current backoff — up to an hour — and the doc that told them otherwise would have them hunting a second fault that is not there. Pinned by `test_a_refusals_backoff_does_not_depend_on_its_classification` in `tests/workers/test_source_revisions_drain.py`, so the correction cannot quietly regress into the old claim.

**That lag was real, and it was not NOPERM's** — every transient class reached the same cap, and nothing shortened the wait when the condition cleared. Closed by #291: a drain pass that publishes anything calls `clear_backoffs`, which pulls every failure-delayed row (`attempts > 0`, not dead-lettered, not touched by this pass) forward to now. One accepted `XADD` is evidence about the *broker*, which is precisely what the waiting rows lack, so the batch already holds what it needs — no probe, no new operator knowledge. Recovery is now the next minute's tick rather than up to an hour. The rows this pass touched are excluded: a row that failed beside a success learned something about itself, not about the broker, so it serves the backoff it just earned. That exclusion is one pass rather than a guarantee — a row that keeps failing while its siblings publish is pulled forward again next pass, and retries at the drain's cadence instead of its own. That is acceptable here and would not be everywhere: publish-phase failure on this stream is broker-wide by construction, since every row `XADD`s the same stream with uniform fields and a row's own fault raises in the build phase, which dead-letters before a publish is attempted. The bulk write is also self-limiting — a cleared row is due, so the predicate stops matching it — which is why a recovery costs one update and not one per tick. Both queries are served by `ix_pending_archiver_sync_due` — `(next_attempt_at, id)`, partial on `dead_lettered_at IS NULL`: before it, the minute-by-minute `select_due` and this clear both scanned the table, which is free at its usual size and is not free at the backlog size an outage produces. The `id` is there because the clear itself creates the hard case: one timestamp across the whole backlog is one sort group, so a single-column index would read every backed-off row to return one batch, on the pass right after the recovery.

**The other three publish paths need nothing, now verified rather than inferred.** They do not classify, and for a denied publish their existing behaviour is already right — `content.fetch` leaves the row `pending_publish` with no ceiling on **all three** of its publish paths (`_issue_fetch_command`, the sweep, the reaper's `_reissue`, each driven separately: the reaper is the one that runs during recovery, and a denial is the one cause guaranteed to still be in force on its next pass), `content.fetch-policy` and `info.watch-status` let the denial escape so the next tick republishes the whole set. `tests/workers/test_broker_acl.py` drives each under `NOPERM`.

**The other half of D3 — the `requirepass` — is already covered, by inheritance rather than by intent.** A bad password or a failed `AUTH` raises `AuthenticationError` / `AuthorizationError`, and in redis-py both subclass `redis.exceptions.ConnectionError`, not `ResponseError` — so `_TRANSIENT_PUBLISH_ERRORS` catches them through its connection entry and never had the NOPERM gap. That is a fact about redis-py's taxonomy, not a decision this repo made, so `test_broker_acl.py` pins the MRO: a future reparenting under `ResponseError` would reopen exactly the hole #290 closed, silently.
